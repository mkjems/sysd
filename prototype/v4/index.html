A design system built on the grid. The principles of Josef Müller-Brockmann, rewritten for a page that scrolls and a screen of any size.

01

The grid

Everything is measured in lines. One line is the body text size multiplied by its leading. Margins, gutters, spacing, control heights and the leading of every heading are multiples of that line.

Paper has one size; a screen has any. So instead of fixing positions, sysd fixes relationships. Twelve columns on a desktop become eight on a tablet and four on a phone, and every layout declares how many columns it takes at each width. On a phone, almost everything falls into a single reading column, but the measurements don’t change.

Press G to show the wireframe: the tinted bands are the columns, every block placed on them is outlined, and images become crossed boxes.

Text that is pleasant to read

A grid is not a cage. It is an agreement between everything on the page about where things begin and how far apart they stand. Once that agreement exists, the reader stops noticing the layout and starts noticing the content — which is the whole point.

“Typography is a service,” as the saying goes. On the web we finally have the tools to perform it properly: lines that break where a typesetter would break them, hyphenation in narrow columns, numbers that line up in a table, and quotation marks that hang outside the edge of the text so it looks straight.

06

Form

Three forms printed over the layout like transparent inks. They are placed on the same columns and lines as the text, so even at their loudest they agree with it. Where they cross, the colours multiply.

Circle,
square,
triangle.

Overprinting is the oldest trick in the print shop. Two inks laid on top of each other don’t cover — they mix, and a third colour appears for free. Red over yellow gives orange, blue over yellow a green, all three together something close to black.

On screen the same thing is a blend mode. Each form is an element with mix-blend-mode: multiply, positioned on the section’s column lines. Text underneath stays dark and readable, because multiplying black by any colour is still black.

The forms are the first rule that is meant to be broken. They ignore the reading column, cross the gutter and run off the edge of the page — but their edges still start and end on the grid, which is why the result looks composed rather than random.

In dark mode there is no paper to print on, so the forms add light instead: the blend becomes screen and overlaps grow brighter rather than darker.

The quiet authority of the grid

Why a layout built from a single measurement feels calmer, reads faster and survives any screen.

Fig. 1 — The figure spans eight columns and twelve lines. On a phone it takes the full width and eight lines.

Order is not the opposite of expression. It is what makes expression legible.

Most pages on the web are assembled rather than composed. Each part arrives with its own idea of spacing, its own type sizes and its own notion of where the edge is. The result is not chaos exactly, but noise: a constant low-level effort the reader spends on working out what belongs together.

A grid removes that effort. When every element starts on a column line and every gap is a whole number of lines, relationships become visible without a single border or background. Things that are close belong together. Things that line up are related. The eye learns the system in seconds and then stops looking at it.

The print designers who developed these ideas worked on a fixed page. We don’t have one. But the principle survives the move to the screen intact, because it was never about the page — it was about proportion. Fix the proportions and the page can be any size it likes.

That is why this system has so few inputs. Choose a typeface and a reading size, and the rest follows: the line, the columns, the space, the scale. Change the inputs and everything moves together, as a single composition.
