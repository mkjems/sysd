01 / Each section is a pageIts own grid · Shared margins

Every section sets its own grid.

Not everything is twelve columns. A section chooses the grid that suits its content: twelve for careful placement, six or five for collections, thirds for reading, or free columns of different widths.

What every section shares is the edge of the page and the gutter, so the site still reads as one publication.

03 / ReadingColumns as wide as the text needs

On design systems / An essay

A common language, with a personal accent.

Good foundations let different pages feel related while giving each one room to develop its own character.

Every page begins with decisions. A typeface, a colour, the distance between a heading and the words beneath it. Individually these choices seem small. Together they establish how a place feels, long before anyone has read a word.

When those decisions are made with care and used consistently, they become a language. Readers learn what to expect. A familiar rhythm makes a new page easier to understand, and frees attention for what the page is actually saying.

Rules worth breaking

A system that only enforces is a template. The interesting pages are the ones where a rule is visibly bent: a column that starts lower than its neighbours, a heading that changes voice halfway through, a section that turns the lights off.

Those gestures only read as intentional because everything around them is consistent. Break one rule at a time, and break it clearly.

Columns that find their own width

This text is not placed on the grid. It asks for columns no narrower than a comfortable measure and lets the browser decide how many fit: three on a wide screen, two on a tablet, one on a phone. No breakpoints were written for it.

That is the modern part of an old idea. The grid is no longer drawn once for a fixed page; it is described as a set of relationships that hold at any size.

06 / The space betweenTwelve columns · Two voices

Notes on attention / 01

Let the words hold the room.

Some pages need very little. A considered title, a passage worth reading, and enough space around both to let the reader settle in. The composition draws its strength from the relationship between what is present and what is left open.

Here, the main column carries the weight of the page. The narrow column beside it offers a second pace, somewhere to pause without interrupting the argument. On a phone the note simply follows the text.

In the margin

Leave something open.

A short observation can sit beside a longer argument. It does not need to fill the height of the page.
